Data, care, and reuse

A minimal downloadable filmography of 3,458 film-director credits. The file contains only film titles, known film years and filmmaker names.

The CARE Principles and the limits of a filmography

While the Indigenous Cinema Atlas is guided by CARE principles, there are limits and tensions between those principles and the information the website makes public. Many of the catalogues, search results, festivals and institutions from which filmographic records are assembled are not themselves CARE-informed. Their descriptions may reproduce histories of classification, exclusion and appropriation.

On the other hand, converting films and their makers into a CSV or Excel spreadsheet makes cinema into sortable data. Although much of this information already circulates online, its aggregation gives it a new form of authority and makes it easier to reuse outside its original context.

At the intersection of these problems, search engine optimization and English-language search results can recursively reproduce racist definitions of Indigenous cinema, including the conflation of films about Indigenous peoples with films made by Indigenous filmmakers. Neither an online citation nor a spreadsheet necessarily resolves this problem.

The Atlas therefore distinguishes discovery from determination. Its records are provisional, cited and correctable; they are not decisions about Indigenous identity, community membership or territorial authority. The public download is limited to names of films and filmmakers, while fuller context is meant to be encountered through the Atlas, its sources and Indigenous-led media platforms. This is one way to reduce further extraction, not a claim that the Atlas has achieved full Indigenous data sovereignty.

CARE in the authors’ words

Stephanie Russo Carroll and colleagues articulate the tension between open data and Indigenous control in The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (2020). Two passages are particularly important to this project:

“Mainstream values related to research and data are often inconsistent with Indigenous cultures and collective rights. For instance, data historically belonged to the researcher in mainstream settings, a notion that has shifted under open data and open science movements where publicly funded research requires data sharing. Meanwhile, Indigenous worldviews have typically centered ‘people’ and ‘purpose’ through governance processes that emphasize collective ownership and control of data (Carroll, Rodriguez-Lonebear & Martinez 2019; Kukutai & Taylor 2016b; Smith 2016; Snipp 2016).”

— Carroll et al. (2020), “The CARE Principles,” Rationale

“The implementation of the CARE Principles in tandem with the FAIR Principles will result in data that reflect the realities of Indigenous Peoples, be useful for Indigenous purposes, and remain under Indigenous control, while promoting knowledge discovery and innovation.”

— Carroll et al. (2020), Conclusion

CARE calls attention to Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility and Ethics. The Atlas seeks to be guided by these principles while acknowledging that collecting and displaying online records is not the same as giving Indigenous peoples control over their data.

Download film and filmmaker names

One CSV with just three columns: film_title, year and filmmaker. A co-directed film can appear more than once, once for each director. The year is left empty when the source does not establish one; nothing is inferred. No affiliation, land, language, theme, country or identity-source fields are included.

Film titles and filmmakers · 3,458 credits (CSV)

More detailed information remains accessible through the Atlas's individual records and, for now, portions of the site's publicly accessible source files. The names-only download is a limit on bulk export offered here, not a guarantee that older public data copies have disappeared.

When possible, visit filmmakers' own websites and Indigenous-led platforms. Filmmakers, communities and other visitors may submit a correction without providing a name or email address.

How to cite the Atlas
