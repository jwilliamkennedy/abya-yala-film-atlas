Grounding Indigenous Cinema through land and relation

The Indigenous Cinema Atlas is a project that facilitates exploring and relating Indigenous film, television, and visual media in Abya Yala and Turtle Island. It does so through the relationship between land and cinema. The Atlas approaches Indigeneity as a political and relational analytic rather than an ethnic signifier, while respecting the authority of Indigenous communities and creators to define the terms of their own identities beyond the purview of any overarching determination (Storfjell 2021; Picq 2026).

The purpose of this project is to enable you to explore films and media from Indigenous creators and communities and learn more about how anthropological histories have often sought to “speak for” Indigenous and Native communities through film in particular.

Background

My name is John Kennedy Godoy and I am the creator of the Indigenous Cinema Atlas. Currently, I am an assistant professor in the Department of Spanish and Portuguese at the University of Colorado Boulder. I am an Irish-Guatemalan American settler from Pennsylvania whose family is from Guatemala (Ixim Ulew) by way of Spain, Scotland, and Ireland. I created the Atlas to help bring attention to Indigenous films so that people can find, watch, and enjoy films made by Indigenous creators.

I was inspired by my Spring 2026 graduate seminar on Indigenous Cinema in Abya Yala and Turtle Island and the films the class watched for the course. I was also inspired by Mirada Nativa ↗, Kin Theory ↗, Katahirine ↗, and other websites that draw critical attention to Native and Indigenous Cinema, terms I use interchangeably here.

In recent years, Indigenous cinema has exploded, including Indigenous television shows like Reservation Dogs. But too often Indigenous representation by non-Indigenous directors and producers is conflated with the shifting definitions of the term “Indigenous Cinema.” While this website doesn’t propose a definition for Indigenous cinema, its eventual goal is to serve as a forum where Native and Indigenous directors can share their own definitions and critiques of the term.

In the meantime, the Atlas gathers Indigenous and Native Cinema in all its forms—documentary, narrative feature-length, experimental, hybrid, or videographic and short form. Did you know that Native Cinema had a silent era? Or did you know that some of the first audiovisual Indigenous films included the voices of children and their drawings as in Alanis Obomsawin’s Christmas at Moose Factory ↗ (1971)?

Work in Progress

The website team does its best—but sometimes people, film festivals, and other websites misrepresent or misunderstand claims of Indigenous relationality and kinship. While the website is designed to push back on that, the reality is that this project is still in its beta stage, trying to find ways to be indexically and ethically accurate.

The Indigenous Cinema Atlas is a work in progress—and it is currently a beta version. There are several issues the map is still working through, including the boundaries it imported from Native Land Digital, and its lack of recognition for certain Indigenous peoples. Additionally, confirming each director’s identification is a time-intensive process; at present, I am a one-person team and am only just beginning to work with members of the Oyate student group at the University of Colorado. Sometimes external weblinks go dead and sometimes other pathways become changed or altered in one way or another. I do my best to maintain the page.

I want to be sure this project pursues indexicality and citationality with the highest ethical practices, but also in ways that recognize indexical precision has often been the métier of violent scientific practices. This is not an excuse but an acknowledgement that working along the archival grain of a heterodoxical, largely unassembled filmography means accepting that what I find online may not be accurate.

If I get anything wrong in the process, I sincerely apologize; I understand that Native territories and identifications can exist in contention, and that settlers and nation-states have long usurped them for violent, genocidal purposes. I will be updating this atlas quite frequently to correct errors. In the meantime, I sincerely hope you enjoy the atlas; I will be working to ensure that publicly available films on Vimeo and YouTube, in particular, along with other websites, are easier to find here thematically, historically, and so on.

The Map

On the map, filmmakers and collectives are placed on the land of the nation or community that they name. Sometimes creators and production teams have more than one affiliation. Placement doesn’t mean a place of residence, citizenship, or a territorial claim—rather, it’s a way to browse and relate films.

The land names used here are from the open-source Native Land Digital ↗, in addition to other Indigenous autonyms and Indigenous scripts. Larger names hold more linked filmmakers while smaller ones remain under research. Thumbnail images show films from land.

Constellate

The purpose of the map is not to localize Indigenous creators but to enable thematic and intertextual comparative exploration and analysis through land, encouraging familiarity with Indigenous autonyms. While many of the films placed on land speak to place, many do not. The Constellation function thus seeks to create connections and thematic harmonies between disparate filmic archives across land beyond questions of geopolity, allowing you to explore filmic connections in Abya Yala and Turtle Island more comprehensively.

A film can have several threads, several themes, several connections. A film can be short, long, or even two images. Threads enable exploration of all these varieties.

My inspiration here is to trace how Native or Indigenous storytelling occupies the space of film—how can film emplace not only ancestral knowledge but also the lives, languages, and lands of people who have long made it? And how can we honor the interconnectivity of filmic practices that relate the myriad voices and politics of Abya Yala and Turtle Island?

Drawing on Jodi Byrd, the website encourages a practice of reading mnemonically: “a reading practice [that] understands indigeneity as radical alterity and uses remembrance as a means through which to read counter to the stories empire tells itself.” As Ethan Madarieta writes, this “means reading the ways and means by which empire requires indigeneity not as a landed presence but as a radical alterity, a constitutive Other, the production of the ‘Native’ by which states produce sovereignty.”

The idea of Constellate then is to read mnemonically through and beyond genre not only to counter the stories empire tells itself but to read along and against the grain of Indigenous media—its complexities, its vitalities, its ground relationalities, its refusals to be contained by generic intermediation without being reified as other, or as different.

As you draw on the map, creating themes, you’ll be able to press play and explore film posters and enter and explore filmographic information and meta-data, even collating information so you can go to your local library to request anything not available online.

Ethics

Cartographic Ethics

In the history of the European conquest of the Americas and Africa, cartography was often part and parcel of the transatlantic slave trade, royal dispensations, and other biopolitical structures—ayuntamientos, land grants, cadastral surveys, and so on. For that reason, maps are often not merely scalar representations of land; they indirectly and directly reconstitute colonial violence.

On the other hand, drawing on Métis scholar Chris Andersen, I also recognize that cartography and storytelling have long been used in many communities in what we presently call the Americas; I resist here the impulse to impute epistemological difference to any community. Rather, I want to recognize that indexicality, citationality, and cartography can be ethically imbricated in projects that uphold, honor, and acknowledge Indigenous sovereignty, land back, and agency. Through filmographic research, the Indigenous Cinema Atlas proposes to be a map of many maps. It is guided by this principle—that Indigenous cinema in the Americas is beautiful and inspired and very much worth sharing.

Filmographic Ethics

Another ethical issue is that film financing can be fraught, exclusionary, and problematic. Too often emerging directors are underfunded and overworked, and that is inflected indirectly here because the map implicitly relates to historic dynamics in which wealthier countries like Canada, the United States, Chile, and Mexico, for example, might have more national funding schemes for filmmakers.

Similarly, film festivals themselves are quite often on stolen lands. And sometimes films never make it to the light of day for a myriad of reasons that reflect legacies of violence and more quotidian realities around the difficulty of production.

Evidence and Care

I am keenly aware and troubled that sometimes people claim Indigeneity when they are not Indigenous or Native, enrolled or in any real, verifiable way hold kinship with the original peoples of Abya Yala and Turtle Island. While there is little my team and I can do about that, our methodology tries to correct for that by working through explicit claims of Indigenous identity, tracing a director’s affiliation from their words and clear bibliographic and filmographic information.

Affiliation comes from the filmmaker’s own words or an explicit biographical source. The atlas does not infer a filmmaker’s Indigenous identity from a film’s subject, language, collaborators or setting. In fact, in 20th-century anthropology, such a practice was often equated with the term “Indigenous Cinema,” and it continues to inform everything from Search Engine Optimization patterns to filmographic and bibliographic study of Native and Indigenous film. At the same time, sometimes multiple Indigenous communities, whether in the Amazon or Oaxaca or Canada, engage in filmic production and characters or stories chart multiple, concurrent tribal and native communities—so collating all that information requires care, attention, and an open-source practice.

The Atlas is careful about historic claims of “descent” for multiple reasons, and where such a term is found in historical film archives it is not used as an affiliation claim.

Every record keeps its sources. Themes come from each film’s published synopsis, which is linked. The atlas does not map ceremonial or sacred sites, and its themes do not describe ceremony.

CARE Statement

Following the ethics of the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019; Carroll et al., Data Science Journal, 2020), this project holds that information about Indigenous people should put Indigenous rights and sovereignty ahead of open access alone—open access in and of itself is only one waypoint for ethical engagement, not the end.

Collective Benefit. The atlas exists as a corrective in and of itself—it seeks to interrupt definitional conflation of Indigenous cinema with more fraught visual ethnographic and voyeuristic documentary production. The end point for the founder of the project is to steward it towards Native and Indigenous digital governance and to enrich the work of educational programs at the University of Colorado Boulder and beyond.

Authority to Control. Filmmakers and communities name themselves here, typically through attested and verifiable claims of Indigenous relationality and affiliation provided in funded, academic, or filmic contexts where the presumption is that certain filters have already been handled. However, historically, such claims were not always handled well, and so the priority is generally to trust information provided by film festivals from recent years (post-2016) in which Indigenous affiliation is linked to workshops, grants, and fellowships for Indigenous directors and filmic collectives. It’s not a perfect method—but it is recursively self-improving. We offer the ability to be removed from the website to anyone for any reason and to correct any entry by anyone to the degree that a correction is made in the spirit of the project.

Responsibility. Only Indigenous filmmakers and collectives whose identity has multiple touch points and filmographic and bibliographic information are listed. There are many leads not yet on the website. We seek to constantly improve this project, following the logic of websites like Mirada Nativa, Kin Theory, and others. This project aims to push back on SEO parameters and spiders that view Indigenous cinema as a flattening analytic by instead centering constellated notions of comparative film analysis and viewership.

Ethics. The atlas is a collation of public information, links, thumbnails, and public domain films. As such, it doesn’t purport to represent cultural knowledge or gatekeep or present anything as though it were an arbiter. It is open to making mistakes and correcting them; it does not prioritize settler-colonial indices, nor does the project seek monetary recompense in the form of honoraria or stipends for its founder, though student researchers are funded where possible.
