Reading Humberto Ak’abal

These are encounters with particular poems by the K’iche’ poet. The design responses below are editorial interpretations, not claims to represent an Indigenous worldview.

“The Pool” and “Memories.” Reflection becomes something held; remembering changes the direction of travel. These poems suggest shifts between overview and close attention, and the ability to revisit a previous encounter. English translations by Earl Shorris and Sylvia Sasson Shorris. Read in Words Without Borders ↗

“Las flores,” “Oración,” and “Libertad.” Flowers carry an account of the earth; benches retain the trees’ presence; birds disregard monumental authority. Their humor and sharp turns resist a uniformly pastoral treatment. Spanish and English, translated by Michael Bazzett. Read in Lunch Ticket ↗

Place names on the map

Place names opens the geographic map. Hover reveals all overlapping records from a 2,059-record Native Land Digital layer distributed by National Geographic MapMaker; click or tap holds the details. Search offers keyboard access. Source names can be ethnonyms or place names and are not all verified autonyms. The imported snapshot was downloaded on 23 September 2026; the service reports a data edit date of 1 October 2023. It is not a current or exhaustive account, and its polygons are not official or legal boundaries.

Ixim Ulew and Iximulew lead to one entry. The International Mayan League explicitly uses Iximulew for Guatemala; the modern country polygon is only a search and hover reference for this usage. Local source territories remain separate. Repeated source IDs are consolidated, while distinct peoples, languages, places, and historical usages remain distinct. Reading the map

Country entry points organize discovery. They do not delimit Indigenous territories or establish a filmmaker’s nationality, residence, or identity. Markers are approximate country anchors, not personal locations. The scope includes 35 sovereign states in the Americas and Puerto Rico.

Reading the lines

Names & lands contains the full supplied terminology collection: 171 distinct entries, with the repeated Nunatsiavut consolidated. The regional groups are reading aids; no individual territorial coordinates are inferred. Each entry retains its supplied associations and provisional usage description. Names alone never establish a filmmaker’s affiliation. The collection also includes the supplied names beyond the Americas.

Hover or focus on a place to unfold its records. Each main branch stands for one matching archive record; the smaller twigs count its captured films and works. The arrangement is a visual interpretation, not a geography of people or territories. The small drawings beside names follow the same record-to-works structure.

The horizontal band includes every supplied name. Its drawn thread follows reading order; the encounter path records your own selections. Colors are editorial choices. Use Appearance to show or hide connecting lines and background flow, or to animate the flow. Your system’s reduced-motion preference is respected.

The original dark atlas and the Linework edition are preserved. This edition adds country textures and colors, coast-following bands, a Caribbean inset, film browsing, and a USA source review. Select a country to keep its branches open; on drawings with up to twelve records, the outlined tips open captured films. The film list offers the same access for larger selections and touch screens.

Reading the evidence

“Map selection” follows the archive’s 155 Map Ready records. This is a research workflow status, not a guarantee of Indigenous self-identification or complete verification. Some legacy catalog records retain unresolved credits, community associations, or possible duplicates. Counts refer to records, not necessarily unique people.

Community references from catalog evidence are distinguished from stronger source-backed affiliations. A film’s subject, language, or location must not be treated as proof of its maker’s identity. Unrecorded affiliations remain unrecorded.
