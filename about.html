About the atlas

Mapping Indigenous cinema through land and relation

The Indigenous Cinema Atlas makes filmmaking across Abya Yala and Turtle Island discoverable through two forms of mapping: geography and cinematic relationships. It begins with Indigenous directorial and collective authorship, not the presence of Indigenous peoples on screen.

Background

My name is John Kennedy Godoy and I am the creator of the Indigenous Cinema Atlas. Currently, I am an assistant professor in the Department of Spanish and Portuguese at the University of Colorado Boulder. I am an Irish-Guatemalan American settler from Pennsylvania whose family is from Guatemala (Ixim Ulew) by way of Spain, Scotland, and Ireland. I created the Atlas to help bring attention to Indigenous films so that people can find, watch, and enjoy films made by Indigenous creators.

I was inspired by my Spring 2026 graduate seminar on Indigenous Cinema in Abya Yala and Turtle Island and the films the class watched for the course. I was also inspired by Mirada Nativa, Kin Theory, and other websites that draw critical attention to Native and Indigenous Cinema, terms I use interchangeably here.

In recent years, Indigenous cinema has exploded, including Indigenous television shows like Reservation Dogs. But too often Indigenous representation by non-Indigenous directors and producers is conflated with the ambiguity of the definition of the term “Indigenous Cinema.” While this website doesn’t propose a definition for Indigenous cinema, its eventual goal is to serve as a forum where Native and Indigenous directors can share their own definitions and critiques of the term.

In the meantime, the Atlas gathers Indigenous and Native Cinema in all its forms—documentary, narrative feature-length, experimental, hybrid, or videographic and short form. Did you know that Native Cinema had a silent era? Or did you know that some of the first audiovisual Indigenous films included the voices of children and their drawings as in Alanis Obomsawin’s Christmas at Moose Factory (1971)?

The Indigenous Cinema Atlas is a work in progress—and it is currently a beta version. There are several issues the map is still working through, including the boundaries it imported from Native Land Digital, and its lack of recognition for certain Indigenous peoples. Additionally, confirming each director’s identification is a time-intensive process; at present, I am a one-person team and am only just beginning to work with members of the Oyate student group at the University of Colorado. Sometimes external weblinks go dead and sometimes other pathways become changed or altered in one way or another. I do my best to maintain the page.

Methodology

I am keenly aware and troubled that sometimes people claim Indigeneity when they are not Indigenous or Native, enrolled or in any real, verifiable way hold kinship with the original peoples of Abya Yala and Turtle Island. While there is little my team and I can do about that, our methodology tries to correct for that by working through explicit claims of Indigenous identity, tracing a director’s affiliation from their words and clear bibliographic and filmographic information. We do not infer or conflate a film’s subject matter, language, setting, or collaborators and production with Indigenous identity. In fact, the whole purpose of this project is to offer a corrective to places where that happens, where the term Indigenous cinema is used to describe films by settlers and non-Indigenous people in which Native people appear, something fairly common to see in any web search.

We welcome corrections, input, interest, and guidance, and our data is open-source.

Cartographic Ethics

In the history of the European conquest of the Americas and Africa, cartography was often part and parcel of the transatlantic slave trade, royal dispensations, and other biopolitical structures—ayuntamientos, land grants, cadastral surveys, and so on. For that reason, maps are often not merely scalar representations of land; they indirectly and directly reconstitute colonial violence.

On the other hand, drawing on Métis scholar Chris Andersen, I also recognize that cartography and storytelling have long been used in many communities in what we presently call the Americas; I resist here the impulse to impute epistemological difference to any community. Rather, I want to recognize that indexicality, citationality, and cartography can be ethically imbricated in projects that uphold, honor, and acknowledge Indigenous sovereignty, land back, and agency. Through filmographic research, the Indigenous Cinema Atlas proposes to be a map of many maps. It is guided by this principle—that Indigenous cinema in the Americas is beautiful and inspired and very much worth sharing.

Filmographic Ethics

Another ethical issue is that film financing can be fraught, exclusionary, and problematic. Too often emerging directors are underfunded and overworked, and that is inflected indirectly here because the map implicitly relates to historic dynamics in which wealthier countries like Canada, the United States, Chile, and Mexico, for example, might have more national funding schemes for filmmakers.

Similarly, film festivals themselves are quite often on stolen lands. And sometimes films never make it to the light of day for a myriad of reasons that reflect legacies of violence and more quotidian realities around the difficulty of production.

Personal Reflection

My inspiration here is to trace how Native or Indigenous storytelling occupies the space of film—how can film emplace not only ancestral knowledge but also the lives, languages, and lands of people who have long made it? And how can we honor the interconnectivity of filmic practices that relate the myriad voices and politics of Abya Yala and Turtle Island?

I want to be sure this project pursues indexicality and citationality with the highest ethical practices, but also in ways that recognize indexical precision has often been the métier of violent scientific practices. This is not an excuse but an acknowledgement that working along the archival grain of a heterodoxical, largely unassembled filmography means accepting that what I find online may not be accurate.

If I get anything wrong in the process, I sincerely apologize; I understand that Native territories and identifications can exist in contention, and that settlers and nation-states have long usurped them for violent, genocidal purposes. I will be updating this atlas quite frequently to correct errors. In the meantime, I sincerely hope you enjoy the atlas; I will be working to ensure that publicly available films on Vimeo and YouTube, in particular, along with other websites, are easier to find here thematically, historically, and so on.

How the atlas works

Filmmakers, films, land

Each filmmaker or collective is placed on the land of the nation or community they name. A filmmaker who carries more than one affiliation appears on each of those lands. Placement does not record residence, citizenship or a territorial claim; country names are only a browsing path.

Land names follow Native Land Digital and Indigenous autonyms, with Indigenous scripts retained. Larger names hold more linked filmmakers. A film image beside a name is drawn from that land’s films and changes as you return.

Theme-weaving

A constellation brings distinct stories into a relationship open to interpretation, and its nodes can belong to other constellations. Theme-weaving puts that principle on the map: a film becomes a point in several interpretive pathways, and choosing a pathway changes which relationships appear. Visitors can move from a film to its geographic context and back without losing the path they followed.

Every line carries a short reason. The atlas distinguishes the kinds of evidence behind a connection:

Woven thread · a pathway through several films, in a star color
Solid line · a connection with a cited source
Dream thread · an interpretive connection, offered as a reading

The weaving language is abstract: lines, braids, knots. It does not reproduce the textile designs of any community.

Disappear map removes the cartography. In a weave, films are laid out by relation instead: by thread order, by year or in a circle, with closeness following shared themes. Each card keeps its people and land. On the main atlas the same button keeps the land names and leaves out coastlines, borders and shading.

Evidence and care

Affiliation comes from the filmmaker’s own words or an explicit biographical source. The atlas does not infer a filmmaker’s Indigenous identity from a film’s subject, language, collaborators or setting. Every record keeps its sources. Themes come from each film’s published synopsis, which is linked. The atlas does not map ceremonial or sacred sites, and its themes do not describe ceremony.

On the map
Reviewed records, and discoveries with a sourced directing credit.
In research
Records whose credit or affiliation is still being confirmed. They are searchable but not shown on the map by default.
Land links
Sourced when an affiliation source names the land; provisional when the filmmaker’s stated nation matches a land name in the map.

Thousands of Indigenous films remain outside the present inventory. The atlas is an experiment and a beginning, not a completed archive.
